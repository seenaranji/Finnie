# Cost Basis: The Starting Point for Gains and Losses

This article summarizes IRS Topic No. 703 (Basis of Assets), checked on 2026-10-31.

## What basis is
Basis is generally the amount you paid for an asset. You use it to figure the gain or loss when you sell, exchange or otherwise dispose of property, and for items such as depreciation.

## Purchased property
In most cases basis equals cost, and "cost includes sales tax and other expenses connected with the purchase." For stocks and bonds you buy, basis is the purchase price plus costs such as commissions and recording or transfer fees.

## Property you did not buy
For stocks and bonds you did not purchase, basis is usually the fair market value on the transfer date or the previous owner's adjusted basis. Gifts and inheritances have their own rules; the IRS refers readers to Publication 551.

## Adjusted basis
Events during ownership can change basis. You increase it by items such as the cost of improvements that add to the property's value, and decrease it by items such as allowable depreciation and insurance reimbursements for casualty and theft losses.

## Why it matters
Basis is the starting point for measuring taxable gain or loss. A capital gain is generally the sale price minus basis, so keeping accurate records of what you paid, and of later adjustments, keeps the figures right. Return of capital payments from a stock also reduce basis (see the article on dividend taxes).

## Where to read more
The IRS points to Publication 551 (Basis of Assets), Publication 550 (Investment Income and Expenses) and the Schedule D (Form 1040) instructions.
