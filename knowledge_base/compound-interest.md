# Compound Interest

Compound interest is interest earned on both your original money and on the interest that has already been added. Over time, this makes growth speed up.

## Simple vs. compound interest
Suppose you put $1,000 in an account that pays 5% a year (an illustrative rate, not a promise).
- **Simple interest** pays 5% of the original $1,000 each year: $50 every year. After 10 years you have $1,500.
- **Compound interest** adds each year's interest to the balance, so next year's interest is calculated on a larger amount.

| Year | Balance with compounding |
|---|---|
| 1 | $1,050.00 |
| 2 | $1,102.50 |
| 5 | $1,276.28 |
| 10 | $1,628.89 |

After 10 years, compounding gives about $128.89 more than simple interest in this example.

## The formula
Future value = P x (1 + r)^t, where P is the starting amount, r is the yearly rate as a decimal (0.05 for 5%), and t is the number of years. Check: 1000 x 1.05^10 is about 1,628.89.

## What makes it grow faster
- A higher rate
- More time. Starting earlier matters because the growth builds on itself
- Adding money regularly

## Rule of 72
A rough shortcut: divide 72 by the yearly rate to estimate how many years it takes to double. At 6%, about 72 / 6 = 12 years.

## It works against you on debt
Compounding also applies to money you owe, such as credit card balances, where unpaid interest is added to what you owe.

Real investments do not earn a steady rate every year, and returns can be negative. These figures only illustrate how the math works.

This article is general education, not advice about what you personally should do.
