# Retirement Accounts Overview (401(k), IRA, Roth)

This article describes general rules for common U.S. retirement accounts as of 2026-10-30. Tax rules change, and the details depend on your situation, so check the official source before acting. Yearly contribution limits are deliberately not listed here, because they change; the IRS website publishes the current numbers.

## 401(k)
A 401(k) is a retirement plan offered through an employer. You choose an amount to contribute from each paycheck. Some employers add matching contributions. Plans usually offer a menu of funds to pick from. Many plans offer a traditional option, a Roth option, or both.

## IRA
An IRA (individual retirement account) is one you open yourself, not through an employer. It can hold investments such as stocks, bonds, and funds.

## Traditional vs. Roth: the core difference is when taxes apply
| | Traditional | Roth |
|---|---|---|
| Money going in | Often pre-tax (may lower taxable income now, subject to rules) | After-tax (no deduction now) |
| Growth | Grows without yearly tax on gains inside the account | Same |
| Money coming out in retirement | Generally taxed as income | Generally tax-free if the rules are met |

The idea: traditional means a possible tax benefit now and taxes later; Roth means taxes now and potentially tax-free withdrawals later.

## Other points to know
- **Rules and limits:** accounts have yearly contribution limits, and Roth IRAs have income-based eligibility rules.
- **Early withdrawals:** taking money out before retirement age (generally 59 and a half) can bring taxes and penalties, with some exceptions.
- **Required withdrawals:** traditional accounts generally require withdrawals starting at a certain age.

Which type fits someone depends on personal details such as income, taxes, and goals. A licensed tax professional can advise on that.

This article is general education, not tax or investment advice.
