# How the Unemployment Rate Is Measured

This article is based on a Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) page that shows a last-modified date of October 8, 2015, checked on 2026-10-31. The ideas are long-standing, but check BLS for current survey details.

## Where the number comes from
The unemployment rate comes from a monthly survey called the Current Population Survey (CPS), conducted every month since 1940 and drawing on about 60,000 eligible households each month. Interviewers ask about what each household member did during the survey's reference week.

## Who counts as unemployed
A person is unemployed if they do not have a job, have actively looked for work in the prior 4 weeks, and are available to work. Active methods include contacting employers, sending résumés and answering job ads. Passive methods, such as just reading job postings, do not count. People waiting to be recalled from a layoff are counted as unemployed even if they are not searching.

## Who counts as employed
People who did any work for pay or profit during the reference week, and people who had a job but were temporarily away from it, such as on vacation or sick.

## The calculation
The labor force is employed plus unemployed people. The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed people as a percentage of the labor force.

## Not in the labor force
People who are neither employed nor unemployed are outside the labor force. The page notes that many are students or retirees, and that family responsibilities keep others out.
