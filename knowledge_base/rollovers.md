# Rollovers From Retirement Plans

A rollover is when you withdraw cash or other assets from one eligible retirement plan and move some or all of it into another eligible retirement plan within a set time. This article summarizes IRS Topic No. 413, last updated 2026-10-08, and checked on 2026-10-31.

## Tax treatment
A rollover is generally not taxable, but you must still report it on your federal tax return. Rolling money into a Roth IRA or designated Roth account from another type of plan is an exception and can be taxable.

## Direct vs. indirect
- **Direct rollover:** the payer sends the money straight to the new plan or IRA. No mandatory tax withholding applies.
- **Indirect rollover:** you receive the money yourself and then deposit it into another eligible plan. The 60-day rule and withholding rules apply.

## The 60-day rule
"You have 60 days from the date you receive it to roll it over to another eligible retirement plan." The period can be postponed if you were affected by a federally declared disaster, and the IRS describes self-certification and waiver options for people who miss the deadline.

## Withholding
For employer-sponsored plans, a taxable distribution paid to you "is subject to mandatory income tax withholding (generally at a rate of 20%)," even if you plan to roll it over. To defer tax on the full amount, you would need to add funds from other sources equal to the amount withheld.

## Other points
- Some distributions cannot be rolled over, including required minimum distributions, hardship distributions from employer plans, and certain loan offsets.
- If you are under age 59½, the taxable portion you do not roll over may face a 10% additional tax, unless an exception applies.
