# The Consumer Price Index (CPI)

This article is based on the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) CPI questions and answers page, checked on 2026-10-31.

## What it measures
The Consumer Price Index tracks "the average change over time in the prices paid by consumers," which makes it a common gauge of inflation in everyday living costs.

## The market basket
The CPI follows a market basket, a fixed set of goods and services that stands in for what households buy. BLS builds it from spending records provided by families and individuals. "The market basket includes everything from food items to automobiles to rent." BLS sorts the purchases into more than 200 categories, grouped under eight major headings such as food and beverages, housing and medical care.

## How it is used
- It is the most widely used measure of inflation.
- Social Security payments receive automatic annual cost of living increases tied to the CPI.
- It is also used to adjust tax brackets, eligibility for some assistance programs and many wage agreements.

## What it does not capture
- **Investments:** the CPI does not include investment items such as stocks, bonds, real estate and life insurance, because they relate to saving rather than day-to-day spending.
- **Some taxes:** income and Social Security taxes are excluded, while sales and excise taxes tied to specific purchases are included.
- **Your own experience:** "The CPI does not necessarily measure your own experience with price change." It reflects an average household, so your own inflation rate may differ.

This article is general education, not advice about what you personally should do.
