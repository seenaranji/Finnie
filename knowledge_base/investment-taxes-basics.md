# Taxes on Investment Gains and Losses (Basics)

This article explains the general U.S. federal rules for gains and losses when you sell an investment, based on IRS Topic No. 409 and checked on 2026-10-31. Tax rules change and depend on your situation, so check the official source or ask a licensed tax professional before acting. It does not cover state taxes, dividends, or interest.

## What is a capital gain or loss?
Almost everything you own and use for personal or investment purposes, such as stocks, funds, or a home, is a **capital asset**. When you sell one, the difference between your **basis** (generally what you paid for it) and the amount you sold it for is a gain or a loss.
- Sell for more than your basis: a **capital gain**.
- Sell for less than your basis: a **capital loss**.
- Losses on personal-use property, such as your home or car, are not tax deductible.

## Short-term vs. long-term
How long you owned the asset decides which kind of gain or loss it is.
- Held **more than one year**: long-term.
- Held **one year or less**: short-term.

The count starts the day after you acquire the asset and includes the day you sell it. Some assets, such as gifts and inherited property, have special rules.

## How each is taxed, in general terms
- **Short-term gains** are taxed as ordinary income, at your regular tax rates.
- **Long-term gains** may qualify for lower rates than ordinary income. The long-term rates are 0%, 15%, and 20%, and which one applies depends on your taxable income and filing status. The income thresholds are updated, so see IRS Topic No. 409 for the current figures.
- Some gains, such as collectibles (coins or art), are taxed at higher maximum rates.

## What if you have more losses than gains?
If your capital losses are bigger than your capital gains, you can use up to **$3,000** of the extra loss to reduce your income in a year ($1,500 if married filing separately), according to IRS Topic No. 409. If your net loss is larger than that limit, the remainder can be carried forward to future years.

## Accounts matter
How a gain is taxed can also depend on the kind of account that holds the investment. For example, retirement accounts such as a 401(k), traditional IRA, or Roth IRA have their own tax rules (see the retirement accounts articles).
