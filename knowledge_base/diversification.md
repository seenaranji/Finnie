# Diversification

Diversification means spreading your money across different investments so that one bad result does not hurt you too much. The common saying is "don't put all your eggs in one basket."

## A simple example
Imagine two people. One puts everything into a single company's stock. If that company struggles, they lose heavily. The other splits their money across many companies, plus some bonds. If one company struggles, the others may balance out the loss.

## Ways to diversify
- **Across companies:** own many stocks instead of one or two.
- **Across asset types:** mix stocks, bonds, and cash, which tend to behave differently.
- **Across industries and regions:** avoid having everything in one sector or one country.

## Correlation: why "more holdings" is not always "more diversified"
Two investments are correlated if they tend to rise and fall together. Holding ten technology stocks gives you ten holdings, but they may all drop at once. Investments that move differently from each other diversify better.

## Funds make it easier
One ETF or mutual fund can hold hundreds of investments, which spreads risk without buying each one separately.

## Limits of diversification
It reduces the risk tied to individual companies. It does not remove market risk: in a broad downturn, many investments fall at the same time. It also cannot guarantee a profit or prevent loss.

## Spotting a lack of diversification
A very large share of a portfolio in one stock, one sector, or one type of asset is called concentration, and it means results depend heavily on that one thing.

This article is general education, not advice about what you personally should do.
